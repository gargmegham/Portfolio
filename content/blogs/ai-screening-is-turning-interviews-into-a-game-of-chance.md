If you are preparing for a job switch or planning to appear for interviews, you might start encountering more AI-based screening rounds than before.

More companies are using AI voice models for initial screening. On the surface, this looks like a simple improvement: faster scheduling, lower recruiter load, and a more scalable way to process large applicant pools.

But after going through one of these interviews recently, I am not sure the current implementation is solving the right problem.

## The Interview Felt Too Mechanical

The interview asked good questions. That part was not the issue.

The problems were in the way the conversation was conducted.

The pacing was unnaturally fast. It interrupted too much and often did not wait for me to finish answering the question it had just asked. Sometimes it would simply move on to the next question before the previous one had landed properly.

At other moments, the opposite happened. It would start waiting for an answer when it had not asked anything in the first place, and I had to tell it to move forward in the interview.

It also kept rambling about things that did not matter. There were moments where it sounded like it was talking to itself, the way someone might talk inside their own head while figuring out what to do next.

That is a strange experience when you are trying to present yourself clearly in a high-stakes setting.

## The Bigger Problem Is Not Just Voice AI

This is not only about one awkward interview flow.

I recently spoke to a recruiter with 20 years of experience in this space. They ran an experiment with around 100 recruiters and five different AI models. The task was simple: choose the best candidate out of three based on their resumes.

The result was surprising.

The AI models flagged resumes that most recruiters preferred as fraudulent. Instead, they picked the resume that almost none of the recruiters chose.

The recruiters followed a pattern most people would consider reasonable. They preferred candidates who showed clear, quantifiable impact.

The AI appeared to optimize for something else. The problem is that we do not really know what that something else was.

## The Screening Loop Is Getting Weird

This brings us to an odd place:

- AI is writing resumes.
- AI is taking interviews.
- AI is filtering resumes.

At some point, predictability starts to vanish. Initial screening begins to feel less like a structured evaluation and more like a game of chance.

Candidates are no longer just trying to communicate their experience to a human. They are trying to produce signals that an opaque system might interpret correctly.

Companies are no longer just trying to identify strong candidates. They are also trying to trust systems whose reasoning may not match the judgment of experienced recruiters.

That is a fragile setup.

## Why Companies Are Still Doing This

The reason is easy to understand.

Most roles receive hundreds, sometimes thousands, of applications. Recruiters cannot manually screen every candidate with the same level of depth. Hiring teams need leverage, and AI promises exactly that.

So the question is not whether companies should use automation at all.

The harder question is what kind of automation actually improves hiring, and what kind simply moves the bottleneck into a black box.

If the first round becomes too fast, too interruptive, or too opaque, good candidates may be filtered out before anyone gets a real signal.

## Maybe We Need Better Signals

I do not have a clean solution.

But maybe the answer shifts toward signals that are harder to fake or mass-produce.

Referrals are one example. They are imperfect, but they carry context and accountability.

Short video introductions could be another. A five-minute Loom explaining why someone is interested in the role requires effort. It is harder to spray and pray. It also gives the candidate a chance to communicate intent, clarity, and motivation in a way a resume often cannot.

These signals are not perfect either. They can introduce their own biases and accessibility issues if used carelessly.

But they may be more useful than blindly trusting an AI system to decide which candidates deserve a human conversation.

## The Point

AI can make hiring more efficient.

But efficiency is not the same as judgment.

If AI becomes the interviewer, the resume writer, and the resume filter, companies need to be very careful about what they are actually optimizing for.

Otherwise, the first step of hiring may become less about finding the right person and more about guessing what the machine wants to hear.
