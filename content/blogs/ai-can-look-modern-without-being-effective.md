AI can make a business look modern without making it more effective.

I keep coming back to that.

Recently, I was on a consultation call with a dev team that looked AI-native from the outside.

They had multiple AI tools in place. They had usage mandates across the team. They had new agents lined up for implementation.

On paper, everything looked progressive.

But once we dug deeper, the reality was different.

Most of the team was not actually benefiting from AI.

They were just performing AI adoption.

Work was not getting easier. Quality was not improving. In some cases, things were getting worse.

## More AI Was Amplifying the Mess

The team kept adding more.

- More agents.
- More tools.
- More automation layers.

But the underlying system was still messy.

So AI was not fixing anything. It was amplifying the mess.

By the end of the call, we aligned on something simple, and honestly a bit uncomfortable:

More AI was doing more harm than good.

## The Hard Move Was Reduction

They did something most teams will avoid.

They reduced.

- Fewer tools.
- Fewer agents.
- Clearer decisions on what actually mattered.

Importantly, they involved every developer in those decisions.

That part matters. AI adoption often gets decided at the top and pushed down as a mandate. But the developers are the ones who feel the daily cost of every tool, every workflow change, every extra review surface, and every new source of context switching.

If they are not part of the decision, the rollout may look clean in a planning doc while becoming chaotic in practice.

## They Brought Back Human Checkpoints

They also reintroduced something many teams quietly remove when they go AI-first.

Human checkpoints.

- Reviews.
- Testing.
- Planning discussions.

These were the places where AI had been silently failing.

The issue was not that AI was useless. The issue was that the team had started trusting automation in places where judgment, context, and shared understanding were still required.

In the short term, they slowed down.

Compared to their previous AI spray approach, output looked lower.

But very quickly, something changed.

- Less stress across the team.
- Fewer customer-facing bugs.
- Better alignment between intent and delivery.
- Less rework.

They did not just look modern anymore.

They started working better.

## Why It Worked for Them

The truth is, this worked because they were a small team.

Less than 10 people.

That gave them high visibility and real accountability. When something broke, people could see it. When a workflow created friction, people could talk about it. When a tool was not helping, it was harder to hide behind dashboards and slogans.

In larger organizations, this is much harder.

Incentives are often misaligned.

- Managers push for more features.
- AI usage becomes a metric.
- Token spend becomes a proxy for progress.
- The cost gets pushed downstream.

And developers carry the weight.

- More micromanagement.
- More pressure to use AI.
- More context switching.
- More burnout.

That is how a company can look more advanced while the actual development system becomes more fragile.

## The Strange Split

So now we are in a strange place.

Smaller teams are quietly fixing their systems.

Larger teams are often optimizing for how things look.

One group is asking, "What is making our work worse?"

The other is asking, "How do we show we are using AI?"

Those are very different questions.

## The Question That Matters

AI can help.

But it helps most when the team already understands the friction it is trying to solve.

If the process is broken, AI does not automatically repair it. It may simply make the broken process faster, louder, and harder to reason about.

That brings me back to the original question:

Are you solving friction, or just giving it better branding?
